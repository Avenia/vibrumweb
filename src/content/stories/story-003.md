The Pyrocaster did not start on the bench. It started with [@pyrohyper](https://www.instagram.com/pyrohyper), who came to us with a finished idea rather than a wish list. He had created the whole design himself: a single-cutaway body with a dark burl top fading into black, natural ash showing underneath, black hardware throughout and a headstock that belongs to no other guitar. He did not bring a sketch. He brought renders.

![The original render of the Pyrocaster, front and back](/images/stories/story-001/photo-1.webp)

## The idea

Everything in the concept grew out of one component: the Evertune bridge. Each saddle on an Evertune sits on its own spring, set to balance the tension of the string at pitch. When the tension drifts, with temperature, with hard playing, with a fresh set of strings settling in, the spring takes up the difference and the pitch stays where it was. Once it is set up, the guitar is simply in tune.

The rest of the hardware was chosen with the same care, and chosen early, because on a build like this every part dictates a cavity, a hole or a dimension. Fishman Fluence Modern humbuckers, with push-pull pots to switch their voices. Gotoh tuners. A Schaller Megaswitch. Nothing on the list was a placeholder.

![Render of the body: alder burl under a black burst, Evertune bridge and two humbuckers](/images/stories/story-001/photo-3.webp)
![The headstock, rendered before it existed](/images/stories/story-001/photo-6.webp)

## From pixels to millimetres

A render is a promise. The drawings are where the promise gets precise. We rebuilt the whole instrument as a 3D model and as a full-size technical drawing: the body outline, the neck pocket, the pickup routes, the control cavity, the battery compartment for the active electronics and the Evertune pocket, which goes far deeper into the body than any ordinary bridge route.

![The body drawing, layer by layer: Evertune pocket, neck pocket, pickups, controls](/images/stories/story-001/photo-2.webp)
![The body as a 3D model, before it had a top](/images/stories/story-001/photo-4.webp)
![The same model with the burl top and the ash sides mapped on](/images/stories/story-001/photo-5.webp)

This is where the collaboration with [@pyrohyper](https://www.instagram.com/pyrohyper) really began. Over the months that followed we went back and forth on every detail: the proportions of the headstock, the shape of the fret markers, how far the burst should reach before the natural ash shows at the edge. Making sure the finished guitar matched it was a conversation that did not stop until the strings went on.

## Wood

The body is a single piece of ash. No centre seam, no bookmatch, just one blank wide enough for the whole outline, with the grain running uninterrupted from horn to heel. A blank like that, with the right grain and the right weight, is not something you find every day, and we did not rush the choice. Ash is a classic body wood for a reason: it is bright, it is lively, and it carries sustain well, which suits an instrument built to be played hard.

The top is alder burl, cut from the root section of the tree. Burl is where the tree's growth went chaotic: the grain folds in on itself and forms the knots and eyes that give the top its figure. We looked for a piece with the figure spread evenly across the whole surface, so that it would still read clearly under a black burst instead of disappearing into it.

![The single-piece ash body, cut to outline and clamped on the bench](/images/stories/story-001/photo-8.webp)
![Checking the burl top after it was glued to the ash body and the cavities were cut through](/images/stories/story-001/photo-14.webp)

The Evertune pocket is the part of this build with the least room for error. The bridge module sits inside the body and the string path has to line up with the neck exactly, so the pocket was cut to the tenth of a millimetre, together with the neck pocket and the pickup routes. The control cavity, the battery compartment and the recesses for the cover plates went in the same way.

![The back of the body routed: control cavity, Evertune pocket and battery compartment](/images/stories/story-001/photo-11.webp)
![Test-fitting the first piece of hardware, the output jack in the control cavity](/images/stories/story-001/photo-16.webp)

The neck is laminated from flamed maple, fossilized oak and mahogany. The fossilized oak is bog oak: timber that lay buried in the bed of the Sava river for thousands of years, while the minerals in the water slowly turned it dark, dense and hard. Three woods glued into one blank make a neck far more stable than any single piece, and under a 25.5-inch scale with 24 frets and an Evertune pulling on the other end, stability is not optional.

![The routed body on the bench](/images/stories/story-001/photo-20.webp)
![The laminated neck blank: flamed maple, fossilized oak and mahogany](/images/stories/story-001/photo-7.webp)

The fingerboard is ebony, with a 14-inch radius and 24 jumbo stainless steel frets. The markers themselves are mother of pearl, cut in a shape of their own rather than the usual dots, set into the ebony and levelled by hand.

![The ebony fingerboard on the CNC](/images/stories/story-001/photo-9.webp)
![The slotted fingerboard with the mother of pearl markers set in](/images/stories/story-001/photo-12.webp)

The headstock wears a flamed maple top. The neck profile is what we call a tiny-U, 42 mm at the nut: slim enough to move fast, with enough shoulder to still feel like wood in the hand. The nut is TUSQ, a self-lubricating material that lets the strings always return cleanly.
The truss rod cover is a piece of ebony with the Vibrum logo inlaid in bone. It is a small part on the guitar, but nobody who picks the instrument up fails to notice it.

![The finished truss rod cover: ebony, with the logo inlaid in bone](/images/stories/story-001/photo-17.webp)
