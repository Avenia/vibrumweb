I do not build many acoustics.

Electric guitars are complex in a mechanical sense — routing tolerances, shielding, grounding, tremolo geometry — but the relationship between wood choice and sound is buffered by electronics. You can compensate. You can correct. The pickup is a filter between the instrument and the listener.

In an acoustic guitar there is no filter. The wood is the sound. This makes it harder to design and harder to justify a material choice that is merely beautiful. Everything has to earn its place.

The Driftwood OM started with wood that was beautiful before it earned anything.

## The Top

The commission came through a woodworker in Novi Sad who had sourced a set of European spruce — not Sitka, European — from a sawyer in the Tara mountain region. Two bookmatched halves, tight grain, no runout visible to the naked eye. He had been holding it for two years waiting for the right project.

European spruce is different from Sitka in a way that takes some explaining. Sitka is stiffer and brighter — it projects quickly and rewards players with a strong attack. European spruce is more complex in the midrange, more responsive to lighter touch, more willing to break up at moderate volume. Martin and Gibson both used European spruce in their pre-war instruments. The comparison matters.

I tap-tested both halves before accepting the commission. They rang evenly, cleanly, with a decay I associate with good tops. I said yes.

## The Body Shape

OM — Orchestra Model — is the right choice for a player who uses a pick and plays both strumming and fingerstyle work in the same session. The 14-fret join gives upper-register access. The slightly smaller body than a dreadnought focuses the low-end response. For a spruce top that rewards touch over attack, the OM is the natural form.

The back and sides will be walnut. Not the most traditional pairing — Brazilian rosewood, mahogany, Indian rosewood are all more common against spruce — but walnut has a clarity in the high-mids that I think will complement this particular top rather than compete with it.

## The Name

The client asked early on what we would call it. I said I didn't know yet.

After two more sessions with the wood on the bench, the answer became obvious. The spruce has a silver-grey quality at certain angles — not the yellow-white of new wood, not the brown of aged wood, something between. It looks like wood that has seen weather. It looks like something found rather than made.

Driftwood OM.

## What Comes Next

The top has been joined and is bracing. I am using a modified X-brace pattern — scalloped braces, lighter than standard, positioned slightly forward of the traditional Martin placement. This is the part of acoustic building where the decisions are smallest and matter most.

Back and sides are bent and fitted. The walnut moved more than I expected on the sides — I will note this for the next time I use the species.

The neck blank is cut. Mahogany, single piece, with a Spanish cedar headstock overlay that complements the walnut without matching it exactly.

We are maybe six weeks from stringing.
