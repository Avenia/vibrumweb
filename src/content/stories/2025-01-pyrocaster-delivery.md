Every build ends the same way: a case clicking shut, a handshake, and then the sound of footsteps going down the stairs.

The Pyrocaster had been in the shop for the better part of three months. I knew every scratch on the body before it was levelled, every fret before it was crowned, every grain line under the nitro. You spend that long with a piece of wood and it starts to feel like it belongs here.

It does not belong here. That is the whole point.

## The Last Check

The morning before delivery I did what I always do — a full setup from scratch. String height, intonation, truss rod, nut slots. Not because anything was wrong. Because I need to know the guitar is right before it leaves, and knowing means checking myself.

The Lollar pickups had settled in nicely over the first weeks of stringing. The bridge pickup has this dry, almost glassy quality when you roll the tone back a little — very 1960s session work. The neck pickup is warmer than it looks, given how tight the coil winds feel. Together they work well: separate voices, not compromises of each other.

Action ended up at 1.6mm bass, 1.4mm treble at the 12th fret. Medium tension strings. The owner plays mostly rhythm with some lead — nothing that needs to be feather-light, nothing that should fight back.

## Handing It Over

He played it for about forty minutes in the workshop before we talked price, which is the right order of things. I watched him find the pickup blend he liked (bridge, just slightly rolled off), find the place on the neck where his left hand wanted to sit (around the 5th position), and stop adjusting.

When someone stops adjusting, the guitar fits.

We talked about care: keep it humidified in winter, the nitro will check if the room gets very dry, don't leave it in a car. Standard. He has had guitars before — he knew most of it already.

## What Stays

The workshop is quiet now in the way it gets when a long build is done. The next body blank is already on the bench — a different guitar, different brief, different wood. I am not sentimental about the transition.

But I do remember every guitar I have sent out. The Pyrocaster: swamp ash, hard maple, Indian rosewood, two Lollar coils, a Wilkinson tremolo that was probably overkill for how little he will use it. Nitrocellulose in an off-white that will yellow slowly for the next twenty years.

By the time it looks its best, neither of us will remember this morning.
